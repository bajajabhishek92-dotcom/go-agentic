# Cars, Not Faster Horses: A Commercial Definition of the Agent Era

Sequoia’s AI Ascent framing is intentionally non-academic: stop arguing about AGI checklists and notice that **the shape of software work has changed**. Pat Grady’s keynote metaphor is the teaching device—**faster horses vs. cars**—paired with Konstantine Buhler and Jim Gao’s economic story of a cognitive revolution, and Sonya Huang’s push for companies to **own their intelligence** rather than rent a black box forever.

## Definition: a commercial AGI test

Grady refuses a laboratory definition of AGI. Sequoia studies founders, markets, and businesses. The **commercial / functional test** is practical: if you can dispatch an agent to do a job, and it can recover from failure and persist until the job is done, you are—functionally—in an AGI-like regime for work, even without technical consensus on “AGI.”

That definition is useful because it is falsifiable in a product sense. Demo chat that answers questions is not the test. **Long-horizon agency that finishes jobs** is.

## Mechanism: communication revolutions vs. computation revolutions

Most founders alive today lived through **communication revolutions**—internet, cloud, mobile—where the breakthrough was distributing information. AI, in this framing (Grady credits partner Konstantine), is a **computation revolution**: how information is processed. That sounds semantic until you feel the product consequence: the foundation under your application moves continuously. New capabilities arrive as discontinuous jumps, not only as nicer UX on a stable substrate.

Grady highlights three inflection points:

1. **ChatGPT / pretraining** — the world saw what large-scale pretraining unlocked.
2. **Reasoning models** — a second scaling law around inference-time compute.
3. **Long-horizon coding agents** — agents that can work across extended tasks (he points to the Claude Code moment as the public reveal).

These look like points on a continuum; commercially they feel like a discontinuous change in what software *is*.

## Faster horses vs. cars

Earlier AI applications made people roughly **10–40%** more productive without changing the job—faster horses. The new generation aims for roughly **10–40×** and reorganizes the work itself—cars. You do not ride a car the way you ride a horse, and you do not build a car company like a stable.

Organizational implication: if agents change the nature of work, they also change the nature of the company—team shapes, review rituals, what “headcount” means, and which roles are leverage vs. bottleneck. Pretending cars are horses leads to bolting a chatbot onto a process designed for manual throughput.

## The $10 trillion cognitive-revolution frame

Buhler/Gao’s economic story pairs with the metaphor: the cognitive revolution is likened to the Industrial Revolution—**as big or bigger, and faster**—with a **$10 trillion** (10^13) opportunity framing. Cognitive work sits where physical labor sat during industrialization: an enormous pool of economic activity about to be re-factored.

Two vectors matter for builders:

- **Tech-out (labs):** pushing general capability, models, and platforms outward.
- **Customer-back (application companies):** specializing general intelligence into workflows that produce revenue, retention, and proprietary learning loops.

The specialization imperative—general components plus specialized labor/systems to produce specific outputs—explains why application companies still matter after foundation models exist. Steam engines did not end industry; they forced specialization. Likewise, startups (and startups not yet formed) specialize intelligence into vertical work.

Huang’s “own your intelligence” remarks sharpen the strategic fork: centralized intelligence that sucks in the world’s data exhaust vs. more decentralized ownership where firms keep workflow knowledge, weights, and evals as compounding assets. Cars-not-horses builders tend to choose customer-back moats—workflows, data rights, distribution—over wrapping the latest demo.

## Affordance and diffusion gaps

A recurring Ascent theme: model capabilities are created faster than Fortune 500 deployment can absorb them. Every day the foundation models move faster than enterprise diffusion, the **gap is opportunity**—for products that create paths of least resistance (affordance) and for founders who build from the customer back.

Failure mode: shipping “AI features” that assume users will reorganize themselves around a model release. Cars need roads, fueling, and new driving norms—not a saddle with a motor.

## Failure modes

- **AGI-debate distraction.** Waiting for a technical definition while customers already buy job-finishing agents.
- **Horse-speed product roadmaps.** Optimizing for 20% productivity skins instead of workflow reinvention.
- **Lab-demo moats.** Wrapping a frontier model with thin UI while competitors own the workflow and data.
- **Static architecture.** Systems that cannot absorb a new model release become liabilities when the floor keeps moving.
- **Ignoring diffusion.** Building only for AI-native teams and leaving enterprise affordance (permissions, audit, change management) unsolved.

### Practice checklist

- Use the commercial test: can an agent recover and finish the job—not merely chat about it?
- Design for 10–40× workflow change, not only 10–40% assistive speedups
- Build moats customer-back (workflows, proprietary data, distribution), not demo-forward
- Assume the capability floor keeps moving; keep architecture model-swappable
- Close affordance gaps: permissions, audit trails, escalation, and deployment paths enterprises will actually use
- Track diffusion lag as a product opportunity, not only as “enterprises are slow”
- Decide explicitly what intelligence you own (evals, weights, environments) vs. rent
