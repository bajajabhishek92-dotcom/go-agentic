# States, Enterprises, and Startups: Demand-Side Power

Labs and chip firms dominate headlines; **governments, large enterprises, and startups** shape what gets deployed, funded, and allowed. Their roles are demand-side and rule-setting as much as invention. Ignoring them produces strategy that looks coherent on a model leaderboard and fails in procurement, compliance, or distribution.

## Governments as market players

States act in at least four ways:

1. **Buyers and hosts.** Defense and civilian agencies purchase models and cloud; sovereign compute projects and “AI factory” style initiatives attract campuses. The AI Index tallies large national investment pledges (semiconductor funds, national AI budgets, Gulf-scale initiatives) that move real capacity.
2. **Regulators.** Risk rules, privacy/ADM laws, chatbot statutes, biometric definitions, and export controls change product roadmaps—covered in depth in the global-policy theme.
3. **Industrial strategists.** Talent visas, research funding, open-model posture (CSET), and chip subsidies are competition policy by another name.
4. **Standard-setters.** NIST’s AI RMF and EU harmonized standards influence procurement language worldwide even when parts of those instruments are voluntary.

CSET’s unpacking of U.S. national AI policy frameworks shows Washington trying to **preempt fragmented state AI laws** while signaling congressional priorities (including child safety). China’s government-sector large-model guidelines (CSET translation) show the state as a **coordinated deployer**—encourage uptake for services and social governance, discourage fragmented stacks, insist on hierarchical alignment and security framing. Treating “government” as only a brake misses customers, hosts, and standard-setters.

Export controls deserve special mention: they make ministries of commerce and customs into AI market makers by shaping who may buy which accelerators—the same geography Epoch’s chip-import explorer quantifies.

## Enterprises: where lasting value accrues

Enterprises convert models into workflows: claims processing, coding assistance, customer operations, clinical decision support, document generation. The AI Index’s jump to 78% organizational AI use marks when this role matured. Enterprises contribute:

- **Proprietary data and process knowledge** that fine-tunes or grounds systems.
- **Integration budgets** (identity, ERP, DLP, SIEM) that often dwarf model API fees.
- **Risk ownership**—they face regulators, unions, and customers when systems fail.
- **Make-vs-buy choices** (closed API vs open weights vs joint ventures) that allocate revenue across the stack.

Epoch’s observation that much workplace AI use still rides free consumer plans outside science and tech roles shows a transitional stage: shadow IT precedes governed platforms. Builders who only sell “innovation theater” lose when security and procurement catch up; builders who offer admin controls, logging, and eval hooks win the second wave.

## Startups: specialists, wedges, and acquisition fuel

Startups rarely train true frontier base models. Their typical roles:

- Wrap frontier models in **vertical agents** (legal ops, healthcare admin, DevOps, sales).
- Sell **eval, observability, security, data pipelines, and guardrails** tooling.
- Exploit distribution niches hyperscalers underserve—IEEE Spectrum’s reporting on a Kenyan startup rethinking AI for small-business mobile-money contexts is one pattern among many global niches.
- Move fast on UX while labs move slow on packaging.

Venture cycles amplify hype and compress timelines; durable startups either own a workflow, a regulatory-compliant niche, or a critical infra tool. Many become features inside hyperscaler or lab roadmaps—exit via acquisition is part of the market design, not a moral failing.

## How the roles fit together

| Player | Primary role |
|--------|----------------|
| Frontier labs | Raise capability ceiling; ship APIs/agents |
| Hyperscalers | Provide compute, power sites, distribution |
| Chip/infra | Manufacture physical capacity |
| Open-weight ecosystems | Adaptability, localization, price pressure |
| Enterprises | Deploy, integrate, own operational risk |
| Startups | Specialize and speed experimentation |
| Governments | Fund, buy, host, and set rules |

Misreading any row as “the” AI industry produces bad partner choices—especially for builders picking clouds, model vendors, or go-to-market paths.


## Procurement as a governance instrument

When enterprises and governments write NIST AI RMF language, logging requirements, or eval evidence into RFPs, they reshape the market more quietly than statutes. Startups that anticipate those questionnaires—model cards, incident response, human oversight design—sell faster into mature buyers. Demand-side power is exercised in contracts, not only in legislatures.


## Labor and delegation

Epoch’s finding that roughly one in five U.S. workers delegates tasks to AI instead of to other humans reframes enterprises and workers as co-evolving market players. Job design, training, and union or professional-body rules will shape which startup and lab products stick. Demand is not only “companies buy APIs”; it is people changing how work is assigned.



## Professional services and system integrators

Often overlooked “players,” integrators translate lab APIs into industry workflows at scale. In many enterprises they hold more day-to-day influence than model vendors. Count them in partner maps beside startups and hyperscalers.


## Takeaways

- Governments are **customers and industrial actors**, not only regulators.
- Enterprises hold **deployment power and liability**; optimize for their constraints early.
- Startups explore the application surface; few should pretend to be frontier labs.
- Map partners by **role in the stack**, not by brand familiarity alone.
- Shadow AI use is a leading indicator; governed platforms are the lagging one—plan for both.
