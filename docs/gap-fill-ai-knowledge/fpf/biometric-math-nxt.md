# Biometric Math Exemptions: When Is a Template Still Biometric?

Biometric data sits at the front of US state privacy fights. Illinois, Texas, and Washington run standalone biometric laws, and 20+ comprehensive state privacy statutes treat biometrics as **sensitive data** with opt-in consent and assessment duties. Consensus on *protecting* biometrics is rising; consensus on *defining* them is not. FPF’s “It’s Just Math” analysis asks a sharper question: if you convert a face or fingerprint into an “irreversible mathematical representation,” should the result fall **outside** biometric privacy law—even when it still uniquely identifies someone?

## The emerging exemption language

State comprehensive privacy laws typically define biometric data as data generated by automatic measurements of biological characteristics that **are used to identify** a specific individual (Connecticut’s formulation is representative). A growing set of bills add a novel carve-out: information converted to a mathematical representation that **cannot recreate** the original measurement data. The target seems to be one-way template/token systems that support verification without storing raw biometrics.

Novelty matters. Classic debates centered on **identifiability**. The new exemption centers on (1) whether a technical process produced a “mathematical representation,” and (2) whether that representation can be reverse-engineered back into the measurement. No comprehensive state privacy law had enacted the exemption when FPF wrote, but several bills came close—so the drafting fight is live.

## Reversibility is undefined

“Cannot be used to recreate” sounds precise; it is not. Encryption of templates, hashing, cancellable biometrics, and other PETs sit on different security assurances. Without a statutory definition of reversibility, aggressive readings could sweep ordinary transforms into the exemption—or timid readings could leave almost nothing out. FPF flags that commonplace transformations (e.g., encrypted templates) may not match the spirit of the carve-out, while cancellable biometrics might be what drafters intend.

Policy justification splits in two: **breach risk** if raw biometrics leak, and **surveillance risk** if identification enables tracking that harms autonomy, anonymity, or freedom of movement. Irreversibility addresses the first more cleanly than the second. A template that cannot rebuild a face can still power continuous identification in public space.

## Middle ground FPF sketches

If both justifications matter, a middle path is to allow the irreversible-math exception only for **specific low-risk uses** that do not implicate broader surveillance—rather than a blanket exit from biometric duties whenever a one-way transform is claimed. That keeps security incentives for PETs without laundering identification systems out of sensitive-data regimes.

Definitional splits already complicate teaching: some states cover data *used or intended* to identify; others cover data that *can* or *allows* identification. Photographs, audio, and video carve-outs vary. In 2026 those fights collide with multimodal AI, spatial intelligence, accessibility features, face-blurring, deepfake detection, and transcription—tools that derive human-measurement data without always “logging in” a person.

## Classroom drill

Compare three systems: (1) raw face images in a gallery, (2) encrypted biometric templates for badge unlock, (3) a mall analytics camera that outputs irreversible embeddings used to re-identify shoppers across visits. Which should biometric privacy law cover under a reconstruction-focused exemption—and which surveillance harms remain even if reconstruction is impossible? Argue from breach risk and autonomy risk separately.

## Why AI makes the old fight new

Multimodal models blur “photo” carve-outs and “biometric identifier” categories: embeddings used for search, deepfake detectors, and accessibility filters all touch human-derived signals. An irreversible-math exemption written for badge systems can be stretched toward those pipelines unless legislators cabin use. Teach students to ask *what identification work the math still does*, not only whether a face can be rebuilt.


## Drafting hygiene

When reviewing a bill, highlight every appearance of “mathematical representation,” “irreversible,” and “cannot be used to recreate.” Demand definitions, burden of proof, and whether the exemption survives if the template still performs 1:N identification at population scale. Without those clauses, “it’s just math” becomes a silent repeal of biometric duties for any vendor that ships an embedding API.

## Takeaways

- “It’s just math” is a **regulatory strategy**, not a technical conclusion.
- Irreversibility without a definition invites both under- and over-exemption.
- Surveillance risk can survive even when reconstruction risk falls.
- Prefer narrow, use-based carve-outs for PETs over blanket biometric exits.
- Watch multimodal AI: derived human signals will keep testing biometric definitions.
