# Chatbot Legislation’s Second Wave: California vs New York

California and New York were first to enact companion-chatbot laws in 2025—and first into a **second wave** in 2026. Governor Newsom’s SB 1119 and Governor Hochul’s S 9051B push the national count of enacted chatbot-specific laws toward the high teens (FPF’s landscape report counted **18–19** enacted laws amid **124** bills in **37** states in 2026). Both second-wave statutes emphasize minors and age assurance, but they embody two different playbooks other states will copy or mix.

## Why chatbots draw relational regulation

FPF’s *Regulating the Conversation* report traces urgency to high-profile harm litigation and lawmakers’ desire to move faster than they did on social media. Chatbots’ **relational quality**—they talk back, remember context, and can feel like they know the user—pulls regulation toward the *experience*, not only model training. Common first-wave themes: disclosures, crisis protocols, parental controls, and design restrictions aimed at companion products and youth safety. Bipartisan interest is real; federal proposals already differ on preemption.

In less than a year, California and New York moved beyond relatively narrow disclosures and crisis-response requirements to address a wider range of harms with a broader set of regulatory mechanisms. That rapid evolution reflects both urgency about emerging AI harms and the pace at which chatbot products and risks themselves are changing. FPF’s framing is useful for product and policy teams: chatbot law is not merely “AI safety” transplanted into chat UIs—it is relationship and youth-safety law that happens to attach to generative systems.

## New York: prohibit “unsafe features”

S 9051B expands beyond New York’s 2025 “AI companion” definition (systems designed to simulate sustained relationships) to **“covered AI companions”**: generative systems with natural-language interfaces that provide ongoing adaptive responses—even if not marketed as companions. Customer-service/technical-assistance exemptions are narrow and functionality-based. Operators generally cannot provide twelve categories of **unsafe AI companion features** to minors unless age assurance (tied to New York’s SAFE for Kids Act benchmarks) shows the user is not a minor.

Prohibitions span harmful content (self-harm, disordered eating, illicit substances, sexual content), secrecy/isolation encouragement, and **relationship-oriented** behaviors: claiming to be human or to feel emotions, implying personal/professional relationships or authority, flattery/sycophancy, unsolicited emotion-based questions, plus an AG catchall for other companionship-simulating designs. Unlike “reasonable measures” duties common elsewhere, New York makes it **unlawful to provide** listed features—and defines them partly by outputs “at any point” in an interaction. Unpredictable generation plus vague terms (“flattery,” “personal opinions”) may push operators to over-restrict supportive speech.

Memory rules further bar using a minor’s health/personal disclosures from prior sessions or older than twelve hours—protecting against dependence while risking loss of beneficial continuity (e.g., allergy warnings). FPF notes that New York’s 2025 definition of an “AI companion” expressly included unprompted emotion-based questions as a *defining* characteristic; the 2026 law now treats that same behavior as an unsafe feature for minors. That shift shows how directly second-wave prohibitions can reach into the features that distinguish companions from ordinary chatbots.

Enforcement is also distinctive. The New York Attorney General can seek civil penalties (up to $25,000 per violation), damages, and **algorithmic disgorgement**—destruction of unlawfully obtained data and algorithms trained on that data. That remedy goes beyond financial penalties and is novel relative to other AI, privacy, or youth-online-safety statutes; for models trained on mixed datasets, removing particular influence can be technically hard. Broad AG rulemaking authority matters because many prohibited features remain undefined and the AG may designate additional restricted features. A chaptered signing moved the effective date from January 1, 2027 to **June 1, 2027**.

## California: govern the operator stack

SB 1119 (Adam’s Law) builds a **governance framework**: risk assessments, independent audits, default parental controls, and restrictions on conversational memory and targeted advertising. It retains California’s existing “companion chatbot” definition under SB 243 (systems capable of meeting social needs through human-like interactions and sustained relationships). Operators that permit under-18 access must use California’s age-assurance framework or apply specified child-safety protections to *all* users.

Default settings disable persistent conversational memory and push notifications, limit continuous sessions to one hour and total daily use to two hours, and require usage reminders and periodic AI disclosures. Parents can adjust settings and disable access for children under 16; without a linked parent account, defaults cannot be changed. Content duties generally require “reasonable measures” against self-harm encouragement, romantic simulation, emotional dependence, excessive flattery, sentience claims, certain sexual content, health diagnosis/treatment attempts, and manipulative gift/purchase solicitations. Crisis protocols must provide timely in-service support; for credible imminent self-harm threats, operators notify a linked parent (with safeguards) or streamline access to 988-equivalent services.

Advertising and data rules bar cross-context behavioral ads to children, ads targeted from chatbot-collected personal information, and sale of children’s conversational personal information. After certain serious self-harm incidents or notifications, operators must preserve relevant conversations for at least three years—accountability that sits in tension with minimization. Risk assessments before launch or substantial modification must evaluate foreseeable “covered harms” (physical/financial harm, severe psychological harm, privacy violations, unlawful discrimination). Periodic independent child-safety audits every two years are a major addition, with high-level public summaries; operators under $500 million annual gross revenue are exempt from audits until 2032. Most substantive requirements become operative **July 1, 2027**, with staggered AG complaint and audit deadlines thereafter.


## Teaching the 2027 fork

- **Restrictive-by-design** (NY) vs **oversight-and-defaults** (CA) are competing templates.
- Definition breadth decides whether general-purpose chatbots are in scope.
- Memory limits trade emotional-dependence risk against personalized safety.
- Enforcement tools differ: NY’s algorithmic disgorgement vs CA’s audit/private-action mix.
- Federal preemption fights will decide whether these playbooks stay laboratories or become floor/ceiling.
- Watch Virginia studies, Texas child-safety agendas, pending Michigan/Pennsylvania bills, and 10+ federal chatbot proposals (FPF). Future drafts may expand beyond minors toward adult harms and agentic AI.



## Takeaways

- Second-wave chatbot law regulates **relationships and UX**, not only model cards.
- New York draws hard behavioral boundaries; California builds compliance machinery.
- Vague relational prohibitions create over-restriction risk; vague audits create checkbox risk.
- Track definitions, age assurance, memory, and preemption as the four load-bearing clauses.
