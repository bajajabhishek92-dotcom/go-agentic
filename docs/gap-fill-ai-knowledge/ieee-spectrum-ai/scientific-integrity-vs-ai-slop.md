# Scientific Integrity vs AI-Written Papers

Peer review was built for scarce human fraud and honest error—not industrial **AI slop**. Spectrum’s reporting—Benjamin Skuse’s “vibe-research” experiment, Harry Goldstein’s trust editorial, and Eliza Strickland’s interview with WikiProject AI Cleanup—makes the integrity problem concrete: it is now easy to generate a plausible fake paper with minimal human labor, and encyclopedias face the same contamination pressure.

## Scale of the contamination signal

Cited estimates include detectable LLM influence in roughly **57%** of published articles by 2025 (up from ~12% in 2023 in a PNAS-linked figure), fully AI-generated reviews in over **12%** of some *Nature Communications* samples, and disclosure rates far below usage (on the order of one formal disclosure per forty papers with statistical AI-use evidence). Hundreds of thousands of fake or AI-heavy publications per year appear in some tallies. IEEE research-integrity manager Amanda Sulicz warns the threat is to **trust in science as a process**, not only individual papers.

Goldstein’s maxim for the moment: **trust but verify**—and verify at industrial scale. Evidence of AI assistance is not automatically “slop”; the integrity target is unverifiable mass fabrication and hollow peer review. Social feeds already normalize machine text; scholarly systems must not.

## The vibe-research war game

Skuse prompted free-tier models (ChatGPT/Gemini flash-class tools in the piece) to invent hypotheses, code, and text with minimal human editing—no paid frontier stacks, no expert prompt engineers, no direct rewriting of model output—then war-gamed submission against *IEEE Access* defenses with managing editor Jenny Mahoney. He chose his old Ph.D. topic (optical solitons) so a sole-author byline would not immediately look fake. Early outputs were thin; section-by-section prompting produced more fluent forgeries. The two-part stake: can an amateur fool publisher defenses with off-the-shelf tools, and what happens when stronger systems also fake the underlying “research”? Features editor Eliza Strickland’s gloss: he basically hit “run experiment” and “write paper,” then launched it into the publishing maw.

Human review plus integrity checkers can still catch free-tool slop—but stronger models make fewer “dumb” mistakes. Publishers are locked in detection vs generation escalation.

## Wikipedia as a parallel gate

WikiProject AI Cleanup (Ellia Lebleu and collaborators, from late 2023) began with obvious ChatGPT clichés—“stands as a testament,” odd “Why is this important?” sections. By 2025–2026, signals became structural (rule of three, negative parallelisms) and behavioral (thousands of words across many articles in minutes). Fake or mismatched citations are a major worry: real DOIs with wrong claims are common even as pure hallucinated citations get rarer with retrieval. Pangram-based estimates flagged on the order of **15–20%** of new articles/drafts as AI-generated in late 2025–early 2026 (conservative lower bound; pre-ChatGPT baselines showed no false positives in their test). English Wikipedia’s LLM-generation prohibition, Paste Check, and AI source verifiers are arms-race tools—themselves imperfect. Some models now refuse Wikipedia-article writing; not all do.

## What practitioners should take away

- Disclosure norms and detection tools are complementary; neither suffices alone.
- “AI-assisted” ≠ slop; target **unverifiable fabrication** and citation laundering.
- Reviewer burden must be matched with better tooling, not only exhortation.
- Institutions that curate shared knowledge (journals, Wikipedia) are infrastructure for reality—not optional prestige layers.
- Watch generation speed and citation fidelity, not only purple prose.

## Disclosure is not enough

Skuse’s war game and Wikipedia’s cleanup show two institutions under the same pressure: fluent fabrication cheaper than review. Disclosure norms help honest authors and do almost nothing against spam mills. Detection tools, human review, citation checks, and generation-speed anomalies must stack. Goldstein’s “trust but verify” at industrial scale is the operating system. Homework: design a journal intake checklist that catches free-tool slop *and* names what still fails against paid frontier models next year.

For classrooms: have students attempt a mini vibe-research abstract on a known false claim and run it through whatever detectors the library provides—then discuss false negatives.

## Takeaways

- Treat integrity as an **arms race** with measurable contamination rates, not a morality play.
- Pair human review with automated checkers—and expect model upgrades to reopen holes.
- Preserve human-curated corpora deliberately; defaults drift toward machine text.
- Trust without verification at scale dissolves the scientific record.
