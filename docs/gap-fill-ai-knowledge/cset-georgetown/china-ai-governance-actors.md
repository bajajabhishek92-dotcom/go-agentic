# China’s AI Governance Proposals: Who Holds Which Duties?

China was early to regulate recommendation algorithms (2021) and frames AI as a **major strategic opportunity** toward world leadership by 2030. CSET’s comparative read of five major Chinese AI governance proposals (2016–2024) focuses less on slogans and more on **which actors get which responsibilities**—the machinery that makes governance real. The finding that travels: older proposals often lack clarity about who governs what; newer ones **name actors and assign detailed duties**.

## Why actor maps beat principle lists

Principles language (“safe,” “controllable,” “people-centered”) travels easily across regimes. Implementation does not. Hipolito Calero’s CSET analysis treats named ministries, regulators, industry bodies, local governments, and enterprises as the unit of analysis because **duties without owners are theater**. For outsiders reading translations, the checklist is:

- Which body owns **licensing**, content controls, safety evaluation, or standards-setting?
- Are **local governments** implementers, experimenters, or both?
- How do **enterprise duties** (model providers, platforms, algorithm operators) interact with state agencies?
- Do proposals create **overlapping jurisdictions**—a predictable friction point when multiple agencies claim the same lifecycle stage?

Dating each proposal matters: China’s AI governance landscape moved from broad strategy documents toward more operational role assignment. Comparative work that ignores dates will invent false continuity.

## Government model-use guidelines as a concrete case

A related CSET translation of **Guidelines for Deployment and Application of AI Large Models in the Government Sector** (internet/cybersecurity regulator guidance) shows the implementer view rather than the international messaging view. The guidance encourages government offices to use AI to speed paperwork, improve surveillance effectiveness, and help predict events such as market swings or disasters—while warning against ad-hoc adoption and urging lower levels to align with systems chosen higher up. It does **not** pick vendors; it regulates **how government may deploy**. Hierarchical consistency and risk aversion sit beside efficiency ambitions.

That pairing is pedagogically useful: China’s “governance” includes both **market/product controls** (algorithms, generative content) and **state self-discipline** about how agencies adopt models.

## Separate strategy from use controls

Teach three layers that often get collapsed in commentary:

1. **Development strategy** — catch up / lead by 2030; industrial and compute goals.
2. **Use controls** — recommendation algorithms, deep synthesis, generative AI content rules.
3. **Government deployment rules** — how public bodies may procure and run large models.

Actor specificity can rise in layer (2) and (3) even while layer (1) stays slogan-heavy. Analysts who only quote the 2017 New Generation AI Development Plan will miss the later machinery.

## How to teach this carefully

- Prefer **organizational charts and obligation lists** over preamble poetry.
- Treat translations and secondary analyses as inputs; cite Chinese originals when making legal claims.
- Expect revision: actor maps are snapshots, not constitutions.
- Compare with EU/US debates: China often regulates **deployed systems and platforms** early; Western debates sometimes fixate on frontier labs alone.

## Classroom and practice checklist

Use this piece as a concept explainer, not a news brief. When teaching or briefing:

- Restate the **core mechanism** in one sentence before citing any organization or product name.
- Separate **what is measured** from **what is claimed**—numbers, thresholds, and evaluation designs are the durable content.
- Map at least one **failure mode** (silent fallback, overclaim, approval fatigue, distribution shift, or missing evidence chain).
- Name the **operator decision** the idea changes: release gating, procurement, curriculum design, facility build, or legal scope.
- Leave students with a **verification habit**: which primary source, diagnostic, or audit would falsify the headline?

These habits travel across geopolitics, privacy law, compute economics, agent harnesses, and applied science stacks alike.

## Takeaways

- Governance capacity is visible in **assigned responsibilities**, not preamble text.
- Recent Chinese proposals trend toward **specificity of actors**—that is the signal to track.
- Government deployment guides reveal state priorities: efficiency, prediction, hierarchical consistency.
- Overlapping jurisdictions are a feature to analyze, not noise to ignore.
