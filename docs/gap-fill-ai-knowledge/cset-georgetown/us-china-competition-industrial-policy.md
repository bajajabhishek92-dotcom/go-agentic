# US–China AI Competition Beyond a Single “Race”

CSET’s competition framing treats US–China AI rivalry as **multi-dimensional**: national security, markets, industrial capacity, and influence over global stacks. Reducing it to one lab leaderboard misses the policy instruments each side actually uses—and misses CSET’s warning that “winning the AI race” without a **theory of victory** may deliver impressive capability without durable economic, military, or diplomatic benefit.

## Action plans as influence documents

US and Chinese AI action plans (2025 vintage in CSET/RAND open-models analysis) mix security language with **ecosystem influence**. The US *Winning the Race* / Action Plan lineage emphasizes domestic execution—compute access for researchers and startups, public–private resource sharing, encouragement of open models “founded on American values,” and export of a full US tech stack (hardware, models, software, applications, standards). China’s *Global AI Governance Action Plan* frames openness and Global South inclusion while advancing Chinese platforms and standards in international fora. Read both as **industrial and diplomatic strategy**, not only safety policy.

## What each side “fears” shapes tools

Commentary such as *What America and China Fear Most About AI* highlights asymmetric anxieties (surveillance and loss of control vs. loss of lead and technological dependency). Those fears help explain instrument choice: export controls and chip geography on one side; indigenous compute, open-model diffusion, and embodied-AI industrial policy on the other. Teach fears as **drivers of policy design**, not as moral scorekeeping.

## Industrial revolution language and theories of victory

*Artificial Intelligence, China, and America’s Next Industrial Revolution* situates AI inside manufacturing, energy, and workforce rebuild—the CHIPS-era story—rather than chatbot demos. Competition is as much about **fabs, talent, and power** as about model releases.

CSET’s *Artificial Intelligence in Competition* brief pushes further: development and deployment are intermediate steps. AI must create a **competitive advantage**, then leaders need a theory of how that advantage becomes victory against adaptive adversaries. Advantage types range from decisive to counterproductive; theories of victory include cooperation, dominance, denial, devaluing, and related strategies. Export controls and secrecy are denial tools; stack export and open-model soft power are influence tools; neither automatically converts into strategic wins if bottlenecks and countermeasures intervene.

## How to teach multi-dimensional competition

- Map instruments across **security / commerce / standards / soft power / workforce**.
- Compare action plans on **openness claims vs lock-in incentives**.
- Ask “advantage → victory path?” before celebrating a benchmark lead.
- Tie model competition to physical industrial policy and talent pathways.

## Classroom and practice checklist

Use this piece as a concept explainer, not a news brief. When teaching or briefing:

- Restate the **core mechanism** in one sentence before citing any organization or product name.
- Separate **what is measured** from **what is claimed**—numbers, thresholds, and evaluation designs are the durable content.
- Map at least one **failure mode** (silent fallback, overclaim, approval fatigue, distribution shift, or missing evidence chain).
- Name the **operator decision** the idea changes: release gating, procurement, curriculum design, facility build, or legal scope.
- Leave students with a **verification habit**: which primary source, diagnostic, or audit would falsify the headline?

These habits travel across geopolitics, privacy law, compute economics, agent harnesses, and applied science stacks alike.


## Theory of victory as homework

Before celebrating a model release or export rule, ask what durable economic, military, or diplomatic outcome the instrument is supposed to buy—and which bottlenecks (fabs, power, talent, standards adoption abroad) could nullify it. CSET’s multi-dimensional framing makes that question mandatory. Soft-power open models and hard-power chip controls can conflict; industrial-revolution language (manufacturing, energy, workforce) is where those conflicts become concrete.

Homework: pick one US instrument and one Chinese instrument; write the victory path each claims and the bottleneck most likely to break it.

## Takeaways

- Leaderboards are one slice; **policy instruments and industrial capacity** are the match.
- Soft power and export controls are complementary—and sometimes conflicting—tools.
- Without a theory of victory, “winning the race” can be a hollow metric.
- Physical rebuild (chips, energy, people) is part of AI competition, not a side quest.
