# Comparing AI Governance Regimes

Global AI policy is not converging on one template. Jurisdictions disagree about **what to regulate** (products, uses, models, or speech), **how binding rules should be**, and **whether safety, innovation, sovereignty, or social control** is the primary goal. A comparative map helps builders and analysts avoid importing the wrong assumptions from a single capital’s debate.

## Four ideal types (and messy hybrids)

| Regime style | Core move | Exemplars in current debate |
|--------------|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Risk-tiered product law | Ban some uses; hard duties for high-risk; light touch elsewhere | EU AI Act |
| Voluntary / framework-first | Soft standards, procurement leverage, sector regulators | NIST AI RMF; elements of UK approach |
| Fragmented legislative surge | Many statutes on chatbots, ADM, biometrics, kids’ safety | U.S. states (+ contested federal preemption) |
| State-steered deployment + content rules | Party-state guidance, platform duties, compute geopolitics | China’s layered AI measures and government-model guidelines |

Real systems mix types. The U.S. pairs NIST frameworks with export controls and a push for federal preemption of state AI laws (CSET’s analysis of the White House National Policy Framework). The UK emphasizes pro-innovation regulator coordination while still engaging frontier-safety institutions. China combines generative-AI content rules with industrial policy and chip constraints.

## What is contested everywhere

Across regimes, the live arguments rhyme even when answers differ:

- **Open vs closed weights.** Diffusion and soft power versus misuse (CSET; Epoch cyber gaps).
- **Preemption vs laboratories of democracy.** Uniform national rules versus state experimentation (U.S. chatbot/ADM wave documented by FPF).
- **Ex ante duties vs ex post liability.** Licenses and conformity assessments versus lawsuits after harm (FPF’s hallucination-in-court mapping).
- **Compute governance.** Export controls and chip tracking (CSET; Epoch imports) versus openness of markets.
- **Definition fights.** What counts as an “AI system,” “high-risk,” or “agent” (EU guidelines; FPF agent taxonomy).

The AI Index’s responsible-AI chapter notes rising incidents alongside uneven adoption of standardized evaluations among industrial developers—evidence that **policy urgency is outrunning measurement infrastructure**.

## How to read a regime quickly

Ask five questions:

1. Does law attach to **use cases**, **model providers**, or **both**?
2. Are duties **binding** or **voluntary**, and who enforces?
3. How are **foundation/GPAI models** treated relative to downstream apps?
4. What is the stance on **biometric and automated decisions** affecting rights?
5. How do **trade and chip controls** interact with domestic AI industrial policy?


## Why definitions decide outcomes

FPF’s agent-taxonomy work and the EU Commission’s guidelines on what counts as an “AI system” show that **definitional fights are substantive policy**. If “agent” means any tool-calling LLM, duties sprawl; if it means only systems that take consequential actions for a user, many copilots escape. If “high-risk” annex categories are read broadly, conformity costs hit startups; if read narrowly, rights protections thin out. Comparative charts that ignore definitions overstate similarity across regimes.

## Enforcement capacity as a hidden variable

Statutes without inspectors, compute for auditors, or technical expertise become symbolic. The AI Index’s note that standardized responsible-AI evaluations remain rare among major industrial developers—even as incidents rise—implies a measurement deficit that every regime shares. Voluntary frameworks (NIST) can outperform unread statutes if buyers enforce them; binding acts can underperform if market surveillance is thin. When comparing U.S., EU, UK, and Chinese approaches, ask who has **budget, talent, and access** to verify claims—not only what the PDF says.

## International forums and soft coordination

Safety summits, OECD principles, UN and African Union framework language (flagged in AI Index governance highlights), and lab accords create a soft layer above national law. They rarely bind like the EU AI Act, but they diffuse vocabulary (risk management, red teaming, transparency) that later appears in statutes and procurement. CSET coverage of AI safety weeks and compute-for-research initiatives shows diplomacy and research infrastructure as governance tools.


## Builder’s comparative checklist

If you ship in more than one region, maintain a living matrix: prohibited practices (EU), high-risk use-case flags, GPAI documentation owner, U.S. state chatbot/ADM triggers, UK sector regulators in play, China content/security review needs, and export-control exposure for your infra. Revisit quarterly—FPF’s chatbot count and CSET’s U.S. framework updates show how fast the matrix changes. The point of comparative literacy is not academic elegance; it is avoiding a single-market product assumption.

## Takeaways

- Compare regimes by **mechanism** (risk tiers, frameworks, statutes, state steering), not by slogans.
- Expect hybrids; “the U.S. model” or “the China model” is usually a bundle.
- Contested issues—openness, preemption, compute, definitions—recur globally.
- Measurement and eval capacity are part of governance, not a separate technical topic.
