# U.S. AI Policy: Frameworks, Statutes, and Preemption Fights

U.S. AI governance is best understood as a **stack of voluntary frameworks, sector rules, state statutes, and contested federal preemption**—not as a single AI Act analogue. Builders shipping nationally feel that fragmentation as compliance load; policymakers feel it as a race between innovation narratives and harm response.

## NIST AI RMF: the soft-law anchor

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) is voluntary guidance aimed at incorporating trustworthiness into design, development, use, and evaluation. It was built through open workshops and public comment, and ships with a Playbook, roadmap, and crosswalks to other standards. NIST continues to extend it—for example, a concept note for an AI RMF profile on trustworthy AI in critical infrastructure—and has signaled revision work tied to broader White House AI action planning.

What the RMF is good for:

- Organizing risk conversations inside enterprises and agencies (**govern / map / measure / manage**-style lifecycle thinking).
- Giving procurement and audit teams a shared vocabulary without waiting for Congress.
- Crosswalking to international frameworks so multinationals can reuse controls.

What it is not: a licensing regime. It does not ban practices or impose CE-mark-style conformity assessments. Its power is reputational and contractual unless agencies or buyers make it mandatory in practice.

## Federal posture: from strategy to preemption politics

CSET’s unpacking of the White House National Policy Framework for AI describes a shift from high-level strategy documents toward **legislative recommendations** aimed at Congress. Unlike purely executive action plans, the framework’s political logic—per CSET—is to establish federal leadership and **preempt conflicting state AI laws**, following failed congressional attempts at state moratoria and a December 2025 executive order calling for a uniform national policy framework.

Important nuances CSET stresses:

- The framework is **not itself regulation**; it is a non-binding call for statutes.
- Priorities blend innovation/energy themes with newly salient **child safety** proposals that poll well and may soften the politics of preemption.
- Free-speech / “ideological bias” arguments appear as justifications for limiting certain state rules—highly contested terrain.

Meanwhile, the AI Index records a surge in federal AI-related regulations and agency activity—more rules from more agencies—showing that “no EU-style Act” does not mean “no federal AI law.” Export controls on advanced chips remain a central federal lever even when domestic product rules stay soft.

## States as the active legislature

FPF’s tracking of chatbot and ADM legislation shows states moving fast: dozens of chatbot-related bills, multiple enacted companion-chatbot laws, Colorado’s Automated Decision-Making Technology Act replacing earlier AI Act experiments, and divergent California vs New York approaches to youth-facing chatbots. That laboratory produces real duties—age assurance, disclosures, human review, prohibited features—while Congress debates preemption.

For companies, the near-term U.S. reality is **multi-state compliance** unless and until federal law clearly displaces it.

## Contested questions

- Should federal law **occupy the field** or set a floor?
- Are companion chatbots a **product-safety** problem, a **privacy** problem, or a **speech** problem?
- How much weight should voluntary frameworks carry in liability and procurement?
- Can independent evaluators (labs granting METR-like access) substitute for regulation?


## Sectoral federal law still binds

Even without a horizontal AI Act, U.S. firms face FTC unfairness theories, equal employment rules for hiring tools, FDA pathways for medical devices, SEC/FINRA expectations in finance, COPPA/youth privacy, and copyright litigation risk. The AI Index’s count of rising federal AI-related regulations across more agencies is the quantitative face of that accretion. “We only follow NIST voluntarily” is incomplete compliance advice.

## Independent evaluation as proto-regulation

Anthropic’s independent-evaluator commitments and White House-adjacent accords on external assessment illustrate a U.S. pattern: when Congress moves slowly, **voluntary deep access for auditors** becomes a legitimacy strategy. Expertise, independence, access, and freedom to publish (Anthropic’s four criteria) are the quality bar. Whether that substitutes for law is contested; as a bridge, it shapes what “responsible frontier lab” means in procurement and press.

## Practical compliance posture today

A realistic U.S. posture for builders: implement NIST AI RMF-style risk processes; map state chatbot/ADM/biometric duties for consumer surfaces; track federal preemption proposals without assuming they will pass unchanged; treat export controls as product constraints for model and infra choices; and maintain eval evidence packs for enterprise buyers who increasingly ask for them.


## Critical infrastructure profiles

NIST’s move toward AI RMF profiles for trustworthy AI in critical infrastructure shows soft law specializing by sector—energy, transport, and similar operators get tailored practices without waiting for a omnibus statute. Pair that with agency-specific rules and you get a quilt: voluntary horizontal framework + mandatory sector patches + state consumer laws. Navigation skill beats wishing for simplification.

## Takeaways

- Treat NIST AI RMF as the **organizational backbone**, not a substitute for hard law.
- Watch the **federal preemption vs state statute** fight as the central U.S. drama (CSET).
- Operational compliance today is often **state chatbot/ADM rules** plus sector regulators.
- Export controls and compute policy are U.S. AI governance even when labeled trade or national security.
