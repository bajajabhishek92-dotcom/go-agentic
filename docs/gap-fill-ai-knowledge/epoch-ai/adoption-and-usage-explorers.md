# Adoption Reality: Usage Explorers, Daily Use, and Task Hand-Off

Capability headlines outrun behavior data. Epoch’s adoption work combines **polling**, **employer-plan patterns**, and a **ChatGPT usage explorer** built from consented chat-history metadata—so debates about impact can cite how people actually use AI, not only what labs ship.

## ChatGPT usage explorer: logs without the anecdotes

Built with YouGov, the explorer publishes **aggregate metadata** from ChatGPT histories of **5,000 US panelists** who used ChatGPT in 2026 and shared exports. Eligibility required opt-in sharing and at least one 2026 use; exports between January and August 2026 yielded about **8.3 million messages** across roughly **660,000 conversations**. Some histories reach back to late 2022, enabling multi-year message-level evolution. Among panelists active December 2022–December 2025, Epoch observes activity in an average of **10.7 months** (median 9); most appear in at least two months, and a meaningful minority span a year or more.

Metadata includes timestamps, character counts, which model replied, and which tools were used—plus demographics for subgroup cuts—without publishing raw chat text. Series default to complete months through December 2025 because 2026 exports are incomplete by construction; incomplete data remains available as an explicit toggle. Why it matters: company blogs and self-reports dominate; independent, multi-year behavioral series are rare. Limitations are first-class—opt-in exporters are not the full user base—but the resource is aimed at researchers and policymakers who need more than vibes.

## Intensity and workplace substitution

Complementary polling insights Epoch highlights:

- **Near-daily AI use** among US adults **more than doubled in six months** in one tracked window—intensity rising, not only ever-tried rates.
- About **one in five** employed US adults report that AI now handles mostly/all of at least one task previously delegated to a coworker or contractor (Ipsos KnowledgePanel; task list drawn from common O*NET activities). Substitution appears across tasks, highest in slices like data analysis, reading documents, and record-keeping—**task-level**, not full job displacement.
- **Most AI use at work happens on free plans**, except in science and tech occupations where employer-provided access is more common—adoption is not the same as enterprise procurement.

## How to teach adoption metrics

- Separate **penetration** (ever used), **intensity** (near-daily), and **reallocation** (tasks handed off).
- Free-plan workplace use means shadow IT and consumer SKUs shape productivity before CIO dashboards do.
- Pair usage explorers with **occupation and demographic cuts**; averages hide science/tech vs everyone else.
- Prefer message-level panels for *how* use evolves; prefer representative polls for *who* uses daily.

## Classroom and practice checklist

Use this piece as a concept explainer, not a news brief. When teaching or briefing:

- Restate the **core mechanism** in one sentence before citing any organization or product name.
- Separate **what is measured** from **what is claimed**—numbers, thresholds, and evaluation designs are the durable content.
- Map at least one **failure mode** (silent fallback, overclaim, approval fatigue, distribution shift, or missing evidence chain).
- Name the **operator decision** the idea changes: release gating, procurement, curriculum design, facility build, or legal scope.
- Leave students with a **verification habit**: which primary source, diagnostic, or audit would falsify the headline?

These habits travel across geopolitics, privacy law, compute economics, agent harnesses, and applied science stacks alike.

## Takeaways

- Build labor-impact arguments on **task hand-off** data, not chatbot download counts.
- Treat independent log metadata as a public good that needs privacy-preserving aggregation.
- Watch free vs paid channels as a map of **who is subsidizing** workplace AI.
- Incomplete recent months are a feature of export timing—read charts with the explorer’s caveats.
